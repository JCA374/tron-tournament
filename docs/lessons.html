Lessons

What this tournament taught, written down as it happens, newest first. Some are about the methods, some about running a two-day experiment unattended.

An opponent you always beat teaches nothing

Thursday 01:30–06:30. PPO, DQN and NEAT were flat for five hours. Their fixed training opponent was the space-filler bot, which PPO beat in 97% of its training games. At 06:39 the territory bot took its place (same share of games, nothing else changed). Two rounds later PPO beat the territory bot 14 times out of 20 (it was 4) and DQN 10 (it was 1). PPO gained about 245 points.

Side effect: DQN then lost 12 of 20 to the space-filler bot, which it no longer practised against. Self-play pools forget easy opponents. Lesson: keep moving the fixed opponents up as the models improve, and keep a few easy ones in the mix.

A yardstick everyone beats every time stops measuring

Thursday 01:32. Round 7 put Mini-AlphaZero up 290 points and PPO up 247 in a single round. But the top four had beaten the pinned space-filler bot 20 out of 20. Against a perfect score the fit only knows "much stronger", and the scale floats: the jump was mostly the ruler stretching, not the models. The order between the models was still sound, because they play each other 20 games per pair.

Fix: a stronger fixed bot (territory) joined in round 8 and became the pinned reference. Lesson: the reference point of a rating scale must still win some games, or it has to be replaced before it stops working.

A process watcher must be tested against itself

Wednesday 22:01–22:05. The supervisor checks with pgrep -f whether each trainer is running. Its pattern began with -m models…, and pgrep read that as one of its own options, so it never found a trainer and started a new copy of all five every 20 seconds. After four minutes about 50 trainers were sharing 8 cores and writing to the same files.

Fix: the pattern is anchored on the full interpreter path, and the supervisor now refuses to run two trainers for one model. Lesson: start the watcher, wait two of its cycles, and count the processes before walking away.

On a CPU, a small MLP beats a small CNN

The first network was a 3-layer CNN over the 15 × 15 window. One training step on 1,024 positions took 345 ms on one core; an MLP over the same 675 values plus the 23 numbers takes 39 ms. Convolutions on tiny inputs don't use the CPU's matrix-multiply speed well. With only one core per model, that 9× difference is worth more than the CNN's built-in sense of space.

Tree search starts far ahead

In a 2-minute test before the start, Mini-AlphaZero, with 16 self-play games behind it, was already level with the space-filler bot. The other four, with thousands of games each, were 130–330 points below it. Searching with an exact copy of the rules finds immediate traps without learning anything. The question for the next two days: do the learners catch up once they've learned what the search sees for free?
